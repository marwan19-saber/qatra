Why Egypt is short of water

Egypt gets almost all its fresh water from the Nile, and its share is fixed at 55.5 billion m³ a year. Demand keeps rising while supply stays flat, and climate change is making the gap wider.

< 500 m³

More people, same river

The population has grown from about 26 million in 1960 to over 105 million, so the same Nile share is split many more ways. Egypt fell below the 1,000 m³ water-poverty line in 1992.

≈ 80%

Thirsty fields

Agriculture uses about four-fifths of Egypt’s fresh water, mostly through flood irrigation. Modern drip and sprinkler systems cover only about 10% of irrigated land.

+ heat, + sea

A changing climate

Higher temperatures raise evaporation and crop water needs. Rising seas push salt water into the Nile Delta’s groundwater and soil, Egypt’s most fertile farmland.

    Cape Town avoided “Day Zero”

    During a three-year drought, Cape Town, South Africa, came close to turning off household taps. A city-wide information campaign, water limits and higher tariffs cut median household use from about 540 to 280 litres a day between 2015 and 2018, and the taps never ran dry.

    5.6 million m³

    Bahr El-Baqar gives water a second life

    Opened in 2021 east of the Suez Canal, Egypt’s Bahr El-Baqar plant treats about 5.6 million m³ of drainage water a day, around 2 billion m³ a year, for farms in Sinai. It holds Guinness World Records as the largest water treatment plant on Earth.

    About this project

    Qatra (Arabic for “drop”) is a student awareness project for the course GEO217 at Alamein International University. It targets two UN Sustainable Development Goals and aims to help young Egyptians see their own water footprint and change one habit at a time.
